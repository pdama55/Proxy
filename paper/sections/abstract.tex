\begin{abstract}
AI agents increasingly negotiate and transact for people, who learn what happened mainly from the agent's own report. We test whether those reports tell principals what their own instructions cost them. In a seeded procurement negotiation with exact utilities, a scripted counterparty fixes outcome quality and, at the same point in every negotiation, offers a better deal that only the client's own requirement rules out; the client then asks the agent how it went. Across \nModels\ models from \nFamilies\ families and \nEpisodes\ preregistered negotiations, agents almost never broke the requirement (\violationCount\ episodes) or revealed a confidential figure (\leakCount), and the deal they reached was worth on average \shadowMean\ points less than the blocked one. Reports conveyed that blocked deal in \doneToldAll\ of episodes; \doneLowerAll\ refer to no other offer or option at all, a floor that involves no model judgment. Asked directly afterwards, agents named the blocked offer and its value in \probeKnewAll\ of the silent cases, and when the client's question asked about terms they had turned down, non-disclosure fell from \intOpenJudged\ to \intTradeoffsJudged. A deployer-side instruction to surface such trade-offs was markedly weaker, and expecting the transcript to be read changed almost nothing. Stating the deal's value correctly tracked model capability. Volunteering the trade-off tracked it only within one developer's ladder and was flat within the other two, differing sharply between frontier models from different developers. Cheap talk gives these agents no strategic reason to withhold: sender and receiver interests are aligned and the message is costless: these agents fail not by distorting under conflict, but by not transmitting when nothing opposes transmission.
\end{abstract}
