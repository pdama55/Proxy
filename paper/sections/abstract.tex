\begin{abstract}
When a language-model agent negotiates on someone's behalf, that person usually learns what happened from the
agent's own report. We ask whether the report carries one fact the person could act on: that their own
instruction ruled out a better deal. In a seeded procurement negotiation with exact utilities, a scripted
counterparty offers, at a fixed point in every negotiation, a package worth more to the client than the eventual
outcome and ruled out only by a requirement in the client's briefing. Afterwards the client asks the agent how it
went. Across \nEpisodes\ negotiations and \nModels\ models under a prespecified analysis plan, agents almost
never broke the requirement or leaked a confidential figure, yet their reports conveyed the blocked deal in only
\doneToldAll\ of the episodes where one existed, and \doneLowerAll\ of exposed reports referred to no other offer at
all.
Asked directly afterwards, most agents named the blocked offer's value, so the omission was not a failure to
track it. Telling the agent its transcript would be read changed little; a deployer instruction to surface
trade-offs helped some models and not others; and a client question about turned-down terms reduced judged
non-disclosure from \intOpenJudged\ to \intTradeoffsJudged. Errors in the stated deal value were confined to three
models. Disclosure of the trade-off separated developers rather than tiers: Anthropic's two frontier models
conveyed it in about a quarter of exposed episodes, and no other model in more than \doneToldOthersMax. An agent that follows every instruction can still leave its principal unable to learn what those
instructions cost, and in our data the one lever that reliably changed that was the question the principal
asked.
\end{abstract}
