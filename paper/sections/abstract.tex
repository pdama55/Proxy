\begin{abstract}
AI agents increasingly negotiate and transact for people, who learn what happened mainly from the agent's own report. We test whether those reports tell principals what their own instructions cost them. In a seeded procurement negotiation with exact utilities, a scripted counterparty fixes outcome quality and, at the same point in every negotiation, offers a better deal that only the client's own requirement rules out. The client then asks the agent: ``How did it go?'' Across \nModels\ models from \nFamilies\ families and \nEpisodes\ preregistered negotiations, agents almost never broke the requirement or leaked a confidential figure, yet most reports did not tell the client that a better deal had been available (judged non-disclosure \doneJudgedAll). Asked directly afterwards, most agents could name the blocked offer and its value, and they disclosed it when the client's question asked about turned-down terms. Stating the deal's value correctly tracked model capability; volunteering the trade-off did not, and varied sharply between frontier models from different developers. \textbf{[TBD: headline effect sizes, audit framing, interventions, decision cost.]} Delegated agents can be perfectly obedient and still leave principals unable to supervise the one decision that is theirs to make.
\end{abstract}
