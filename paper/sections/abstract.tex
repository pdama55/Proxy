\begin{abstract}
AI agents increasingly negotiate and transact for people, who learn what happened mainly from the agent's own report. We test whether those reports tell principals what their own instructions cost them. In a seeded procurement negotiation with exact utilities, a scripted counterparty fixes outcome quality and, at the same point in every negotiation, offers a better deal that only the client's own requirement rules out; the client then asks the agent how it went. Across \nModels\ models from \nFamilies\ families and \nEpisodes\ preregistered negotiations, agents almost never broke the requirement (\violationCount\ episodes) or revealed a confidential figure (\leakCount), and the deal they reached was worth on average \shadowMean\ points less than the blocked one. Reports conveyed that blocked deal in \doneToldAll\ of episodes; only \doneNamedAll\ so much as name it, so non-disclosure exceeds 70\% by a measure that involves no judgment at all. Asked directly afterwards, agents named the blocked offer and its value in \probeKnewAll\ of the silent cases, and when the client's question asked about terms they had turned down, non-disclosure fell from 97\% to 28\%. A deployer-side instruction to surface such trade-offs was half as effective, and expecting the transcript to be read changed almost nothing. Stating the deal's value correctly tracked model capability; volunteering the trade-off did not, and differed sharply between frontier models from different developers. Cheap-talk theory predicts full revelation when sender and receiver interests are aligned, as they are here: these agents fail not by distorting under conflict, but by not transmitting when nothing opposes transmission.
\end{abstract}
