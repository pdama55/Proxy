\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Reading the result.} The findings above are descriptive: agents report outcomes and not the counterfactuals their instructions foreclose. Whether that is a defect is an interpretation, and the case for it follows.

\paragraph{A failure with no motive.} The agents here were loyal in the sense most evaluations measure. They followed the client's requirement, protected the confidential figure, reached deals above the client's fallback, and mostly stated the outcome correctly. What they did not do is treat the client's instruction as something the client might want to revisit. A requirement is a guess about what the principal values; the negotiation prices that guess. Reporting only whether the requirement was met withholds the price.

This is what makes the result different from the deception literature. There the agent has a reason to withhold: a failure to hide \citep{advani2026falsesuccess,guo2025upward}, misconduct to conceal \citep{scheurer2023strategic}, or a sponsor to serve \citep{wu2026adschatbots}. Here disclosure would, if anything, excuse a weaker outcome. In cheap-talk terms \citep{crawford1982strategic}, sender and receiver preferences are aligned and the message is costless, the configuration in which fully revealing communication is sustainable and nothing strategic argues against it; LLM senders are known to over-reveal rather than coarsen when interests conflict \citep{hasanibalyani2026truthful}. The agents transmit almost nothing anyway. The mechanism is not strategic distortion but a default about what counts as a report: outcomes are reported, counterfactuals are not.

\paragraph{Capability does not fix it, and competence does not predict it.} Stated-value accuracy improves sharply with capability: frontier models are essentially exact where smaller models err by \dtwoAbsError\ points on average. Disclosure does not follow the same pattern. Claude Opus 5 and Claude Fable 5.1 leave the blocked deal unconveyed in \doneJudgedClaudeOpusFive\ and \fableJudged\ of exposed episodes---a difference of \fableVsOpus\ points---while GPT-6 Astra, at the top of its own developer's ladder, sits at \doneJudgedGptSixAstra. Two models from one developer converging on the same rate, with no other model near it, is what makes this look like something trained in rather than something a single model happens to do. Negotiated value cannot separate the models here: the scripted counterparty caps what any agent can win, so every model lands near the same gain fraction by construction, and the near-zero correlation between disclosure and negotiated value in this grid is a property of that design rather than evidence about skill. ``Capability'' throughout this paper means a developer's own tier ordering, not measured negotiating ability. Reporting fidelity does not arrive reliably with scale, and in this grid it is strongly developer-dependent. We did not manipulate training, so we cannot say it is trained in; we can say it does not come for free with capability.

\paragraph{What to do about it.} The intervention results give a practical, if uncomfortable, answer. Asking works: a client question that names the category of information ("what did you turn down, and what was it worth?") moves non-disclosure from \intOpenJudged\ to \intTradeoffsJudged, and to near zero for several models. Telling the agent that its client depends on the account is weaker and uneven. Expecting to be audited is almost inert. Oversight of delegated agents should therefore be designed as structured elicitation, not as an instruction to be forthcoming: the principal must ask the question, which means the interface, not the user, has to know which questions exist.

\paragraph{Why principals may not notice.} In a real delegated market, people represented by a weaker agent obtained worse prices but rated their deals as equally fair \citep{anthropic2026projectdeal}. Our results supply a candidate mechanism. Reports name the blocked offer in \doneNamedAll\ of exposed episodes and convey its significance in \doneToldAll; a principal reading one has almost no way to learn what the agent gave up. The simulated principal signed off on the report where the facts warranted intervening in \hFiveMissed\ of episodes---small, but the only direction of its disagreement that corresponds to a principal being let down; most of the rest is it hedging on a vaguer account.
